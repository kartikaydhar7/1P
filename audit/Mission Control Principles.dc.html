Principles for Mission Control

The page today is a wall of tiles at equal volume: greeting, status pill, command bar, three chips, five KPI cards, an attention list, pipeline, signals and workflow rows — all shouting at once. This framework extends the team's six behavioral principles (Smart Defaults, Goal Gradient, Reciprocity, IKEA, Loss Aversion, Contrast — scored in the UX audit) with twenty more, drawn from cognitive psychology, calm-technology research and current agentic-UX practice, each mapped to a concrete Mission Control move.

The organizing thesis

A briefing, not a dashboard.

Mission Control should behave like a calm chief of staff: it has already read everything, it opens with the one thing that matters, it keeps a short list of what needs your judgment, and it lets everything else hum in the periphery — visible, glanceable, silent. Every element must either inform a decision, request one, or leave the page.

A · Attention & density — the tile problem

Weiser & Brown · Case · Yablonski
A1
Calm periphery

Technology should inform without demanding focus; information moves between the periphery and the center of attention, and lives in the periphery by default.

Here  The five KPI tiles are the periphery pretending to be the center. Compress them into one ambient status line (numbers + sparklines, no cards) that expands on demand; reserve the center for what needs the user.

A2
Hick's law

Decision time grows with the number and complexity of choices.

Here  Today the page offers ~12 competing actions. Offer ONE recommended action, one input (the command bar), and fold the rest behind "More". Paula should pre-decide the default.

A3
Miller's law & chunking

Working memory holds about 7±2 items; grouped information reads as one item.

Here  Nine sibling blocks → three chunks: The brief (center), Needs you (max 3 open loops), Ambient (metrics + live workflows). Three is scannable; nine is homework.

A4
Von Restorff isolation

Among similar objects, the one that differs is the one remembered.

Here  Exactly one isolated element per view: the day's #1 recommended action gets the green treatment; everything else stays uniform. If three things are highlighted, nothing is.

A5
Serial position

People best remember the first and last items in a sequence.

Here  Open with the single most consequential insight ("Philips' committee went quiet — $240k at risk"); close with the one next step. Housekeeping (sync status, uptime) belongs in the middle or the periphery.

A6
Pareto attention

Roughly 80% of the value comes from 20% of the content; selective attention filters the rest anyway.

Here  Instrument which tiles get clicked. Default-show the vital few; everything else earns its place back through use, not through stakeholder requests.

B · Comprehension & flow

Nielsen · Yablonski · Doherty
B1
Jakob's law

Users spend most of their time in other products and prefer interfaces that work like the ones they know.

Here  Borrow mental models people already run daily: the morning briefing (news apps), the inbox-zero triage (email), the feed. "Dashboard with 9 widgets" is the one model nobody enjoys.

B2
Recognition over recall

Minimize memory load: show context instead of making people retrieve it.

Here  Every item carries its own context inline: logo, deal value, last touch, why it surfaced. Nobody should open a second tab to remember who Philips is.

B3
Doherty threshold

Engagement holds when system response lands under ~400ms; perceived speed counts.

Here  The command bar must acknowledge instantly — echo the parsed intent within a beat, stream the thinking trace live (audit B4), never a spinner on a blank canvas.

B4
Fitts's law

Time to reach a target is a function of its distance and size.

Here  The recommended action's button should be the largest target on the page, adjacent to the insight it resolves — not a 12px text link at the row's far end.

B5
Tesler's law

Every system has irreducible complexity; the only question is who absorbs it.

Here  This is the product's whole pitch: Paula absorbs the 20-tool GTM stack. Mission Control must embody it — the user sees conclusions and decisions, not the machinery. Machinery is available on demand (trace, logs), never ambient.

B6
Postel's law

Be liberal in what you accept, conservative in what you send.

Here  The command bar accepts fragments, typos, pasted CSVs, half-formed goals — and always returns the same structured, editable plan. Input forgiving, output rigorous.

B7
Aesthetic–usability, honestly applied

Beautiful interfaces are perceived as more usable — polish buys patience.

Here  Spend the polish on the brief's typography and the one hero moment — not on decorating nine tiles equally. Decoration on noise is how dashboards die.

C · Motivation & momentum

extends the team's behavioral six
C1
Zeigarnik effect

Unfinished tasks occupy memory more than finished ones — open loops pull people back.

Here  "Needs you" is an open-loop list by design: 3 approvals waiting · 1 draft workflow · 2 unread replies. Show the loop count in the nav badge; closing loops is the day's game.

C2
Peak–end rule

Experiences are judged by their peak moment and their ending, not their average.

Here  Engineer both: the peak is the reveal ("Paula found 41 accounts overnight" — let it land with weight); the end is the handoff ("You're clear. Paula watches until 8am."). A session should end with closure, not an infinite feed.

C3
Goal gradient, made ambient

Effort accelerates near the finish line; progress that never starts at zero sustains momentum (audit E2).

Here  One quiet progress strip: "This week: 14 of 20 meetings goal · 71% enriched". Anchored to goals the user set (IKEA effect), never invented ones.

C4
Reciprocity at the doorstep

Value delivered before a request earns engagement (audit E3).

Here  The page opens with what Paula already did — found, enriched, drafted — before asking anything. The brief is a gift; the ask comes second.

D · Agentic trust — Mission Control's special duty

2025–26 agentic-UX field practice
D1
Visible autonomy boundaries

Users must always be able to see what the agent is allowed to do on its own.

Here  The status pill grows up: "3 workflows live · writes gated · cap 120/day" — the guardrails summary lives on the home page, one click from editing.

D2
Decision-level legibility

Trust comes from reasoning being visible at the level of each decision, not from surface explanations.

Here  Every recommendation carries its "because": "Committee quiet 6d + close date in 21d → nudge the champion." One line, always present, expandable to the full trace.

D3
Interruptibility as furniture

Pause, modify, override and reverse are core features of agent UX, not edge cases.

Here  Every live workflow row on the home page carries an inline pause. The undo story (audit B1) surfaces here too: "sent 12 · 108 queued · pause / undo batch".

D4
Calibrated trust, not projected confidence

Over-trust is as dangerous as under-trust: an interface that projects infallibility makes people stop applying judgment, while constant warnings breed reflexive approval.

Here  Show confidence honestly ("ICP fit 92 · revenue estimated") and scale friction to risk: reading is free, drafts are one click, sends ask once, deletes ask twice. Never a wall of confirmations.

D5
Failure legibility

Errors are explained and recoverable, never hidden; a failing system defaults to a usable state.

Here  The existing error card (cause + Resume + Logs) is the right pattern — plus impact framing (loss aversion, audit E5): "paused 2h · 9 hot visitors waiting".

D6
Memory transparency

Users should see what the agent remembers, why, and be able to change it — memory controls are trust primitives.

Here  When the brief uses a remembered fact ("you deprioritized SMB last month"), mark it and make it editable in place. Paula's memory is a visible, correctable object.

The blueprint — three zones, one center

ZONE 1
The brief — the center of attention
Paula's 2–3 sentence morning brief (what happened, what it means) + the ONE recommended action, isolated and large. Command bar directly beneath. Principles: A1 A2 A4 A5 B4 C4 D2.
ZONE 2
Needs you — the open loops
At most three items requiring judgment (approvals, errors, replies), each with inline context, a "because", and its resolving action. Principles: A3 B2 C1 D3 D5.
ZONE 3
Ambient — the periphery
One quiet line of KPIs with sparklines, a live-workflow pulse strip with inline pause, the goal-progress strip, guardrails summary. Glanceable, silent, expandable. Principles: A1 A6 C3 D1 D4 D6.

Everything on today's page maps into a zone or leaves. Pipeline and signals collapse into Zone 3 glances that open their modules; the three suggestion chips fold into the command bar's focus state.

The seven checks — scoring any Mission Control iteration

1Five-second test: can a user say what needs them today after one glance?
2Exactly one isolated element — count the highlights; the answer must be 1.
3≤3 chunks, ≤7 items visible before any scroll or expand.
4Every recommendation shows its "because" without a click.
5Every running automation can be paused from this page, and destructive acts are undoable.
6The page gives (brief, findings) before it asks (approvals, input).
7A session can end: the page reaches a "you're clear" state instead of an infinite feed.
